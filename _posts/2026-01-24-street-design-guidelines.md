Janette Sadik-Khan served as the Commissioner for New York City’s Department of Transportation (DOT) between 2007 and 2013. She describes herself as "one of the leading voices on urban transportation policy."

Sadik-Khan was remarkably focused, which is why I believe she succeeded as a leader. Her target was singular: to make New York’s streets safer. In her book, *Streetfight*, she documents her tenure and focuses on how she redesigned New York’s streets. It is unusual to find a Transport Commissioner willing to write about their experiences with such transparency. 

This blog reflects on her "transformative redesigns" and organizes the discussion under three main themes: **Planning, Designing, and Implementing.**

---

## PLANNING

### Produce a Strategic Plan
Under the guidance of Mayor Mike Bloomberg, one of Sadik-Khan’s first acts was creating a strategic plan for the DOT. A **Strategic Plan** is a management tool used to set priorities, focus energy, and ensure stakeholders are working toward common goals.

Unlike typical elusive government documents, this 72-page plan was clear: cut annual traffic fatalities by at least **50%** between 2007 and 2030.

### Reach out to the Public
Sadik-Khan suggests addressing policy enactment through public relations campaigns to prevent residents from being "preemptively spooked by daily headlines." 

However, this point gave me pause. While she advocates for participation, her approach to the Times Square project felt more like "informing" than "consulting." Today, the NYC DOT conducts "pop-up workshops," but it remains unclear if these sessions are truly consultative or primarily a method to manage public perception.

* **Work with community representatives:** In 2007, her team developed a "pocket plaza" at Pearl Street with neighborhood input, providing vital public space for local workers.
* **Bring community artists into the process:** Partnering with neighborhood groups to create murals gives people the opportunity to shape their own streets.

## DESIGNING

### Follow Desire Lines
Great road designs follow people. **Desire lines** are the paths people naturally take, regardless of designated crosswalks. If a crowd consistently crosses a road "illegally," it’s a signal that a crosswalk belongs there.



Sadik-Khan argues that reconfiguring these spaces doesn't need to be costly. A simple application of **paint, gravel, planters, and cones** can transform a street by regulating traffic according to actual human behavior.

---

## IMPLEMENTING

### Experiment, then Implement
To transform streets into plazas, the NYC DOT developed **pilot programs**. These are temporary, short-term projects assessed over weeks or months. 
* **Tactical Urbanism:** This approach tests changes to the public realm using inexpensive, flexible materials before making permanent infrastructure investments.
* **Examples:** Times Square and Herald Square both began as pilot programs before becoming permanent fixtures of the NYC landscape.

### Nothing stops you, until it does
Sadik-Khan highlights a harsh reality: many cities today are not dense enough to make transport investments sustainable. Antidevelopment policies lead to sprawl, forcing reliance on cars.

Furthermore, the **federal gas tax** has remained at 18.4 cents since 1993. Adjusted for inflation, that value has dropped to just **11 cents**, leaving the massive burden of road maintenance almost entirely on the cities themselves.

---

## Personal Thoughts
I find it hard to imagine Sadik-Khan succeeding in a city without the unique density of New York or Los Angeles. Her success was largely bolstered by her immense professional network. As she notes in Chapter 11, her ability to simply "pick up the phone" and reach the heads of the MTA was key to resolving inevitable friction. 

To be successful in urban transformation, you need a high degree of power and influence. Sadik-Khan clearly possessed both, making her achievements—while difficult to replicate—a masterclass in urban leadership.
