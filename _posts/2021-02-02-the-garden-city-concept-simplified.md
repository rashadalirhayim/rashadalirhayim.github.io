Ebenezer Howard developed the garden city concept in London between the 1880s and 1890s. This was during a crucial time when cities were swarming with problems due to overpopulation and congestion. Howard's garden city concept was highly influential and guided the design of many towns across the United States, Germany, and France. The first garden city is Letchworth, followed by Welwyn in Hertfordshire, England. Howard's garden city concept is one of the most influential to this date, and I will attempt to provide a brief explanation here.

## Ebenezer Howard's garden city concept

The principles guiding the garden city concept are not unique and can be found in earlier texts that were circulating at the time. According to Hall (2019), low population density, good housing, wide roads, an underground railway, and open spaces, are concepts that Benjamin Ward Richardson discussed in his 1876 pamphlet Hygeia, or the City of Health. There are other precursors. The list below, from Hall's influential book Cities of Tomorrow (2002), clearly illustrates this:

- The concept of land nationalization: Herbert Spencer
- The purchase of farmlands by a community at agricultural values: Thomas Spence
- Planned colonization: J.S Mill in his influential book Principles of Political Economy
- Most of the features for his Garden City diagram he obtained from James Buckingham's plan for a model town
- The anarchist philosophies of Peter Kropotkin in his Fields, Factories and Workshops (1898), influenced his reasoning towards the industrial decentralization of people from the congested city

In 1898, Howard published the first edition of the concept in his book entitled To-morrow. In this book, some of the essential features Howard describes in the garden city are:

- The city should have a population limit of 32,000
- A permanent green belt of 5,000 acres surrounds the cities; agricultural land Howard stressed a limited-dividend company would own
- Each garden city would connect to others by a rapid transit system

Soon after publishing his first book, in 1899, Howard founded the Garden City Association (GCA) as a platform to discuss his ideas. He also established a development company, Garden City Limited, and hired Barry Parker and Raymond Unwin as architects for Letchworth, what was to become the first garden city.

## The garden city movement spreads

The garden city movement spread across England, Europe, and the United States, however, planners often iterated the designs. Here are some of the most prominent examples in planning influenced by the garden-city movement:

### 1. Clarence Stein and Henry Wright (1928): Radburn Cities

Members of the Regional Planning Association of America, Stein and Wright adapted the English Garden City idea in planning Radburn. They designed houses in clusters with two fronts and no backs to prevent through traffic. The clusters, or 'superblocks,' they connected through cul-de-sacs surrounded by arterial roads, thus resembling the arterial roads in Howard's garden city diagram.

Only a small section of the project was completed because of the great depression. The project was highly influential and provided the basis for almost every American new town from then to 1980 (Hall, 2002).

### 2. Ernst May (1925 – 1930): New Frankfurt

Ernst May was one of the staunchest supporters of the garden city movement. He prepared designs that were modern but followed some of the principles of garden cities, including his use of decentralized planning and community building.

In 1925, Frankfurt's mayor, Ludwig Landmann, selected Ernst May to design and partake in the 'New Frankfurt' initiative. May designed a satellite town in Römerstadt, outside Frankfurt. Although it is not a garden city, it adopted many of its principles.

While the garden cities movement was influential, it did not last. In fact, there are only two instances where the garden city diagram was implemented: Letchworth and Welwyn. Nowhere did governments move to produce the 'real thing'.

In part, this is because Howard's ideas were widely misinterpreted. His diagrams were almost universally understood as merely physical blueprints. Yet, Howard was much more interested in social processes (Hall, 2002). In essence, he advocated for the communal ownership of land.
