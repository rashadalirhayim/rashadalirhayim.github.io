The first time I studied place attachment was when I was working my master's thesis. Initially, my aim was to take an environmental determinism perspective. I wanted to examine how certain building layouts could impact the way people socially interact in a neighborhood.

But after further research, I decided against that impulse and took instead a phenomenological approach. I realized the complexity of the concept warranted a much deeper understanding of its determinants. An understanding that I thought the subjective opinions of the people would best describe.

And so, using this approach, I conducted in-depth interviews in Istanbul to determine how immigrants, displaced by the war in Syria, experienced their neighborhoods. I was curious about the kind of attachments immigrants formed with their neighborhoods in the years they resided in Istanbul.

After concluding my 12-month long study, I wanted to share some of the knowledge I gained from the countless of papers I read. I didn't want to share everything I knew about place attachment and chose instead to focus on its dimensions. This is because, to a large extent, an understanding of place attachment depends on an understanding of its dimensions.

## So, what is place attachment?

A broad definition defines the term as a bond between people and places.

However, similar to emotional attachments between people, place attachments vary by person according to our past experiences and the nature of the place. In environmental psychology this is called, place identity.

The term was first coined by Proshansky in 1983 who observed that the places people interacted in had an effect on shaping their identity over time. This occurs when individuals draw inferences about themselves and cognitions about their environment (i.e. memories, values, and preferences), and express them in their definitions of self.

What attracts us to a place varies of course. Sometimes there is something about the place that attracts us to it. It is quiet. It is quirky. Perhaps it is its openness and spaciousness that we value. Or perhaps a place is special because it brings to mind pleasant memories.

We are all attracted to a certain type of place.

I for one, love my parents' home in Bahrain. I'm not only fond of the house because my parents live there. I love the routine and the simple, slow-paced life I lived in my many years there. It was so hard for me to leave when the time came. I believe it still is. There's a lingering sense of loss that follows me wherever I go. Whenever I can, I try to visit that place.

It is through this 'wanting to go back' we can tell if we are attached to a place or not.

But bonds with place need not be positive. Sometimes a place can negate negative emotions. This is particularly true where upsetting experiences in a place, such as experiences of tragedy and loss, can induce a strong aversion towards these places. Although most times it is not any particular quality in place that has instigated these emotions, it becomes difficult for us to dissociate our feelings with such environments.

## Place attachment, why?

While ensuring residents in a particular neighborhood have positive place bonds is not on the agenda of politicians these days, it certainly is worth looking at some of the reasons why it should be.

Place attachment encourages social engagement and cohesion in neighborhoods. Several studies show that residents who possess stronger attachments to their neighborhoods, are more likely to participate in community events and maintain social ties with neighbors. Satisfactory social ties lead to individual satisfaction and security [1] and ultimately, cohesive neighborhoods.

There are also psychological benefits. Several studies posit that place attachment is necessary for psychological stability. They maintain, 'place is a reference point from which to view the world and a basic source of identity' [2]. And so, our well-being depends on our rootedness to a place.

If place attachment could satisfy social or psychological needs, then its absence would entail both psychological and social losses. And so, place attachment can be used an important indicator of the psychological well-being of residents as well as the social cohesion of neighborhoods.

But is place attachment measurable?

## Measuring place attachment

In general, there are two approaches to the study of place attachment. The first is quantitative and involves using questionnaires and surveys to measure place attachment. The second method is qualitative. Most often qualitative studies rely on interviews, focus groups, and observations to create an in-depth analysis of place attachment. This is often amongst a limited number of participants.

In my study, which I will be sharing soon on this website, I used a qualitative method. This is mainly because I believe people have complex understandings of attachment that are hard to quantify. Also, place attachment has multiple dimensions that we cannot pre-determine ourselves. By allowing participants to inform rather than be informed, I got a more in-depth analysis.

Still, the majority of studies today on place attachment rely on quantitative methods. This has given rise to multiple questionnaires and surveys relying on different interpretations of place attachment. Naturally, the factors determining place attachment among these studies are also widely disparate. I've listed below some of the dimensions [3] I found just to give you an idea:

## Social ties

This is perhaps the most significant and cited factor in studies of place attachment. In fact, up until the 1980s, most studies focused on the effect of social ties only as a determinant of place attachment. They referred to it as 'community attachment'.

Our ability to form and sustain social ties influences how we experience place. Most often through these ties, residents are able to obtain some form of support. This in turn helps create trust amongst neighbors and increases their level of comfort and satisfaction.

Of course, not everyone is able to, or wants to form ties in their neighborhood. One's ability to form ties differs according to many factors, some of which I outline below:

- **Families with children:** Those with children had more social ties than those without.
- **Age:** Older people seem to value social factors more than the physical. Younger people on the other hand favor neighborhoods that enable them to achieve their goals in life [4].
- **Gender:** In several studies, women were shown to maintain more ties with neighbors than men [5].
- **Time spent:** The more time people spent in a place, the more social ties they formed.
- **Professional status:** People that don't have jobs probably have more time to experience the neighborhood than those that do. The pandemic could impact this. It would be interesting to see how.
- **Education:** The more educated people are, the more diverse their network is, and the wider the geographical range.
- **Ethnic diversity:** This is widely contested amongst studies. Arguably, trust, co-operation, and friendship are reduced in ethnically diverse neighborhoods. Whereas homogenous neighborhoods reported greater social ties.

You can see how important the socio-demographic and personal background of a person is in determining place attachment. This is because our experiences, place identity, determine to a large extent how we perceive and value place.

## Physical features of place

Unlike the social component, the physical dimension of place has not, until recently, received scholarly attention. This is because we often regard the physical place as "a container of social processes rather than an independent object of studies".

However, since industrialization, there has been a rising interest in the physical environment as a source of human well-being. Rising problems in the city instigated discussion on the role the physical environment played in fostering social and environmental issues.

Here I'd like to stop and ask a question. If buildings and their surroundings had no effect on the people inhabiting them, then how else would planners shape the environment?

In my opinion, the physical features of a place may inhibit or foster social connections indirectly.

A simple example of this is a tree that offers shade in a park. A passer-by stops and sits under the tree. In the time she sits under the tree, several people pass by. Some meet her eye and smile. Others nod. Some might stop and say hello. It is also possible that none of those happen and the lady is occupied with a book. Regardless of what happens, the environment has not inhibited the opportunity of social interaction to take place.

And so, there are certain physical factors that have been shown to affect people's attachment to their neighborhood. Some of these factors are:

- **Access to landscaped areas:** There are a myriad of studies that demonstrate the importance of access to a shared, outdoor area. Shared spaces offer great opportunities for neighbors to meet and are places where people can relax outside their home.
- **Aesthetic quality:** This relates not only to the aesthetic appearance of a neighborhood, but how well it is maintained. It goes without saying that a well-kept neighborhood is more likely to instigate positive emotions than one that lacks services.
- **Environmental quality:** People who are attached to their neighborhoods appear to view them as less polluted than those who aren't. It is possible, however, that their attachment to their neighborhood masks any environmental problems their neighborhoods experience.
- **Walkability and building layout:** Much of the research on this comes from New Urbanism. An urban design movement that advocates for the design of more walkable neighborhoods for building a better sense of community.
- **Building size**

All the points above are not to be taken without consideration of other factors. The physical features of place vary by socio-demographic, socio-economic, as well as other factors. A study that considers the social and physical factors will have to control for such variances.

Given the above, it's easy to see the wisdom in Forrest's assertion on how to determine place attachment:

> Essentially you can determine if a resident is attached to their neighborhood by knowing two things: who they are and where they are [6].

## A final say

Whether you are an architect, planner, developer, or a person working in the development of communities, your aim is to look for ways to design spaces that are more habitable. Place attachment offers many opportunities for the development of more habitable spaces.

Neighborhoods that report greater satisfaction amongst their residents are safer and more cohesive. Their residents show greater participation in grass-root community organizations and are more environmentally responsible.

However, place attachment is a complex concept with several social and physical dimensions. It is important when trying to study place attachment to consider these dimensions in tandem.
