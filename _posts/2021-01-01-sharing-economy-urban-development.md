Over the last decade, the sharing economy has experienced exponential growth. The promises of more sustainable consumption and socio-economic gains have attracted considerable debate, and for good reason. For cities under stress from rapid urbanization and rising demand, platforms that unlock idle assets seem like an obvious opportunity. But whether the sharing economy will evolve in line with sustainability goals, or simply converge with business as usual, is still very much an open question.

This piece examines both sides of that debate, using home-sharing platforms as the primary case.

## What do we actually mean by the sharing economy?

There is widespread confusion around the term, and the word "sharing" is partly to blame. In everyday usage, sharing does not involve financial remuneration. It is an exchange without economic benefit. The sharing economy, on the other hand, may or may not involve monetary transactions.

A useful distinction separates true peer-to-peer sharing platforms, where people exchange excess goods like BlaBlaCar, from quasi-sharing platforms that do not always involve underutilized assets, like Uber or Airbnb. For the purposes of this discussion, I will use Botsman's definition: "an economic model based on sharing underutilized assets from spaces to skills to stuff for monetary or non-monetary benefits."

## The opportunities

The environmental case for home-sharing rests on a straightforward idea: if people rent out space they are not using, cities need less new construction, which means less energy use and fewer greenhouse gas emissions. Airbnb itself has reported that its users consume 63-78% less energy and 12-48% less water compared to hotel guests. During the European refugee crisis, apps like Refugees Welcome matched displaced families with residents who had spare rooms, a genuine example of the model working as intended.

That said, the actual environmental picture is more complex. Rebound effects complicate the calculation. The additional income generated by hosting tends to get spent on other goods and travel, partially or fully offsetting any environmental gains. The data here is genuinely scarce, and researchers have been candid about that limitation.

On the social side, proponents argue the sharing economy builds trust and social capital through ratings and review systems. This is plausible in principle. But studies have consistently found evidence of discrimination on these platforms. White hosts on Airbnb were found to charge 12 percent more than non-white hosts for comparable listings. An analysis of over 200,000 listings found wide disparities in pricing, ratings, and reviews along racial lines. These are not incidental findings. They are partly a product of how profiles are displayed and how algorithms surface results.

The distributional argument is similarly complicated. In theory, platforms cut out the middleman and spread value more evenly across the supply chain. In practice, data from the New York Attorney General's office found that 6 percent of Airbnb hosts captured over 37 percent of all host revenue in the city, while lower-income residents were largely excluded from the market altogether. That is not distribution. That is concentration.

One alternative worth noting is collective ownership, where hosts hold a stake in the platform and have a say in how profits are shared. Several initiatives along these lines exist, though none have reached significant scale yet.

## The regulatory challenge

Regulators have struggled to keep pace with platforms that grow faster than any policy response can follow. Airbnb operates at the intersection of civil codes, rental laws, safety regulations, and city zoning, a complexity that has allowed it to largely bypass the taxes, licensing fees, and insurance requirements that traditional hospitality businesses face. Critics argue this creates unfair competition and exposes consumers to risk. Supporters counter that heavy regulation stifles innovation and imposes real costs on small operators.

New York City's experience illustrates the tension well. Its short-term rental regulations required hosts to register with the city and be present during guest stays, effectively removing tens of thousands of listings from the market. Opponents accused city officials of protecting hotel industry interests. But those listings also represented housing that had been taken out of the long-term rental market. Both concerns were legitimate.

The deeper problem is that platforms, as they scale and face investor pressure, tend to drift away from the values that made them appealing in the first place. Even non-profit sharing firms face pressure to adopt more commercially oriented models over time. If regulators fail to intervene effectively, the risk is that the sharing economy simply reproduces existing inequalities through a more efficient mechanism.

## Where does that leave us?

The sharing economy presents real opportunities for cities, particularly in optimizing underused assets and reducing the need for new construction. But the benefits are contingent on factors that platforms do not automatically provide: equitable access, genuine redistribution, and alignment with broader sustainability goals.

Some platforms, particularly as they scale, could contribute to more equal and sustainable cities. Others will converge toward the same extractive dynamics that characterize conventional markets, just with a better interface. The difference will largely come down to market structure, regulatory environment, and who has a stake in how these platforms are governed.

The challenges here are not going away. But that is precisely why they deserve serious attention from planners, policymakers, and anyone who cares about how cities develop.
