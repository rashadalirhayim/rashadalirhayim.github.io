Over the past decade, a large portion of the Kingdom's coastline was privatized. In an effort to maximize profits, building and zoning laws were amended to profit from land that was once protected from development. This is clearly seen in many parts across the Kingdom where private development projects (Bahrain Bay, The Avenues, Upscale Residential buildings in Juffair) transformed what was once a place for public gathering to private shopping malls, resorts, and commercial buildings. As expensive hotels and resorts take over most of the country's coastline, the public role of these places begins to diminish.

This public role is particularly important since day-to-day interactions these places encourage, lead to positive aspects such as increased social interaction within communities (Brown et. al, 2003).

On the other hand, privatization not only excludes people from accessing these 'public goods', but also tends to dictate what is deemed acceptable behavior and what is not. In the recent opening of the 'semi-outdoor' shopping center, The Avenues, a by-passer complained from being restricted to bike in the space that was supposedly a public seafront alley. Even the architecture of the place seems unwelcoming, as the lack of benches to sit reflect the lack of choice for anyone seeking to enjoy the sight without needing to pay $5 for a cup of coffee and a place to sit.

Even fishermen have been excluded from these places. Fishing huts which were once a staple of the Bahraini coastline, have since become a rare sight as developments continue to eradicate all forms of its existence (Karimi, 2018).

But what is the public's say in all of this? In 2010, a Bahraini led team brought to attention this phenomenon at an exhibition held in the Venice Biennale. The team investigated the impact of land reclamation on the decline of sea culture and the socio-political changes that accompanied these transformations. While this exhibit won the first place and received great publicity, little has been done to stop this. Today only a handful of these huts can be found in Halat el Seltah and Halat Al Naim (Karimi, 2018).

This however does not exclude that Bahrain has taken a particular interest in providing public parks and walkways. The year 2010 witnessed the opening of two large seafront walkways; The Arad Bay, a 2km walkway which sits right next to a private hotel; and Prince Khalifa Bin Salman Park, also a widely visited public space.

Despite this, with a population of over 1.5 million with the majority living in the capital Manama, the space for public remains insufficient, both in terms of their geographical location and proportion.

**Moving Forward:** There is a need for the public and authorities to realize the importance of these places for social cohesion and integration within communities and maintain the appropriate balance between public and private. Beaches are our common inheritance and if we don't want to lose them, their protection must be our collective responsibility.
