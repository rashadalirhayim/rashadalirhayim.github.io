In recent years, there has been a rising interest in the traditional neighborhood development model, among scholars as well as buyers seeking value for their money. Many people seek the advantages that a walkable and mixed-use neighborhood offers. While others are attracted by the increase in value that a TND promises.

But what does a traditional neighborhood development look like? And why are people willing to pay more to live in those neighborhoods? Also, is there such a thing as a 'traditional neighborhood'?

## What is Traditional Neighborhood Development?

Traditional neighborhood development or (TND) is traditional in the sense that it borrows its principles and ideas from the design of early American town plans, specifically those that were built from the 1600s up until World War II.

The withering of the urban fabric as a result of poor planning and development practices during the post-war era (i.e. sprawl, urban regeneration, gentrification) lead many planners, developers, and engineers to seek alternative planning methods.

The TND approach is one of many models that sought to restore a sense of community to neighborhoods. These models include neo-traditional development, new urbanism, pedestrian pockets, and transit-oriented development.

The term TND was first coined in New Hampshire between 1990 and 1992 when Rick Chellman, Norman Stahl, Andreas Duany, and Scott Brooks sought to write and seek the passage of the first TND zoning ordinance.

The main objective of this ordinance was the adaptation of zoning laws, specifically those that allowed for the prevalence of suburban design and went against the TND design needs.

> Today TND refers to a compact, mixed-use neighborhood where residential, commercial and civic buildings lie within close proximity to each other.
> Smart Growth Manual

## TND, not New Urbanism

Often TND is used interchangeably with 'New Urbanism'. New urbanism is an urban design movement that also promotes walkability and environmentally friendly habits. Moreover, Duany and Chellman, who helped initiate the TND ordinance in New Hampshire, both went on to become founding members of the New Urbanism movement, which today is the Congress for New Urbanism (CNU).

However, it is important that the two terms not be confused. TND deals specifically with the neighborhood, while New Urbanism encompasses all scales of planning and development.

## Principles of Traditional Neighborhood Development

While there is no single model for TND, there are a number of principles that guide most TNDs. These vary according to the sources you consult. The list below is a summary of the principles Duany Plater-Zyberk & Company (DPZ) list on their website. They have written zoning ordinances for several cities including Columbus OH, Dade County FL, and Middleton WI.

- Is compact; designed for the human scale. Suffice to mention here is that the number of dwellings permitted under this section will largely determine the density of the area. Some towns have zones with different zoning ordinances. An example of this is Charlestown PA.

- Provides a mix of land uses that will provide the necessary needs for residents. This includes restaurants, shops, schools, offices and the relevant civic buildings.

- Is diverse. That is, it allows for a variety of housing types (single family dwellings, townhouses, as well as multifamily units) and brings people of diverse ages, ethnicities and incomes.

- Is walkable; has interconnected relatively narrow streets, with sidewalks, bikeways, and multiple transit routes.

- Embedded in the cultural and environmental context of the neighborhood.

- Incorporates environmental features in its design. For instance:
  - Environmental corridors
  - Protected natural areas
  - Community parks; a medium-sized public space for recreation, its landscape should require only limited maintenance. A community garden is an ideal gathering place for residents of apartments and houses and play an important communal role.
  - Sports field: for large-scale recreation. This should be confined to the edge of a neighborhood because 'its size is disruptive to the fine-grained network of the neighborhood'.
  - Streams, ponds, and other water bodies
  - Stormwater detention/retention facilities

- Is consistent with the city's comprehensive plan.

- Reserves its prominent sites for community buildings (e.g. town halls and religious buildings).

- Design features:
  - Hides parking lots, which are instead located in the rear side of buildings.
  - Positions its buildings close to streets.
  - Creates streets that are narrow and shaded.
  - Provides commercial buildings with street-level retail space.
  - Accommodates sidewalks and bike lanes.

## Why do people want to live in Traditional Neighborhoods?

### Location

This is perhaps the most prominent determinant in one's decision to purchase. A place becomes a prime location because of its proximity to factors that people find desirable (i.e. jobs, transportation, landscape, etc.).

TNDs take these factors into consideration. And so, they are more likely to be located in strategic locations, that would in turn increase their value.

### Greater sense of community

I am a bit skeptical about this point but will mention it here for reference. A number of studies, including this one by Bothwell et al, argue 'traditional neighborhoods, such as those found in many cities and inner suburbs, provide their residents numerous opportunities and venues for social interaction'.

The study maintains that well-designed streets and front porches encourage neighbors to come together, forming a 'community'. Of course to come to a conclusion like that you will have to believe that certain environments have the power of influencing people to take certain actions, also known as environmental determinism.

It is possible for certain kinds of interaction to happen on the porches of our homes, across the street, or on the sidewalks between buildings. However, there are so many factors that come into place in the making of a community, it is impossible to make an attribution.

## Is it truly 'traditional'?

There are numerous neighborhood developments that claim to be traditional. The Town Paper has an extensive list of neighborhoods that it lists as 'traditional development': https://www.tndtownpaper.com/neighborhoods.htm.

It is, however, unclear how they have come to their selection criteria, which they have listed on their website. What is clear, is that the term 'traditional neighborhood development' means different things depending on the sources you consult.

What is more, the laws that govern TND differ between states, counties, and cities. Of course, some degree of variation is expected based on how traditional towns are developed (i.e. from scratch or through infill development). Also, to develop a neighborhood that mimics the cultural and environmental context of a city/village, certain aspects must be fine-tuned to meet those requirements.

But there are certain factors, particularly those relating to walkability, compactness, and density, that remain hard to define, let alone find, in many of today's so-called traditional towns. And so, it's hard to think of those towns as traditional.

## Final thoughts

The thing with models like Transit-oriented developments, Traditional neighborhood developments, and even garden cities is that they attempt to provide a 'manual' for city/town development. Most of these principles provide plausible design approaches. For instance, building narrower streets, providing a mix of land and residential uses, and creating multiple transit routes.

However, when we look at other factors like creating more walkable spaces, designing more compact neighborhoods, things start to become more complicated. Like how dense should dense be? What is walkability?

It's important to reflect back and think about some of these models and ask whether they truly are what they claim to be. The case often is that developments that claim the TND label, are not as traditional as they really are. 'Traditional' has become a label which is often used to make towns and villages appear more attractive to those who seek to live in more compact, pedestrian-friendly environments.
